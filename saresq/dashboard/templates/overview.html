The problem, and the idea

After an earthquake or a flood, the decisive task is searching. A drone can cover a collapsed block in minutes, and the value is in turning that flight into answers rather than into an hour of footage.

SaResQ does the watching on the aircraft. A thermal camera says where to look, a visible camera says what is there, and only confirmed finds are transmitted. That design follows directly from the link budget: the radio a small drone carries moves about 16 kilobits per second, where a 28-byte alert lands in 14 milliseconds and its confirming crop follows 2.5 seconds later. The architecture is built around sending answers, and it is why the system works at full radio range.

How it works

  1. 1

    The thermal camera looks for anything warmer than its surroundings

    The comparison is relative, not absolute, so it holds across ambient conditions. It compares every pixel against the median of the frame, scaled by the median absolute deviation, and flags anything 2.5 standard deviations above it. Median and MAD are used instead of mean and standard deviation because a person in the frame would otherwise inflate the very threshold meant to find them.

  2. 2

    Those hot spots become crops of the normal camera

    The thermal array is only 32×24 — a person at 20 m is about 2.6 pixels. That is exactly what is needed to localise a heat source, and the visible camera then supplies the identification. A calibrated affine transform maps the hot spot into the 1640×1232 visible frame, where the same person is roughly 115×31 pixels.

  3. 3

    A neural network judges only those crops

    Running it on six 160 px crops costs 169 ms of a 250 ms frame, leaving 29% headroom. The thermal stage has already done the expensive part — deciding where to spend the compute — which is what makes real-time inference on a £35 computer practical.

  4. 4

    A second network reads the scene as a whole

    Separately, once a second, the whole frame goes to a classifier that says whether this is a flood, a fire, a collapsed building, a traffic incident, or a normal scene. A survivor in a flooded street and one on dry ground are different dispatch decisions.

  5. 5

    Only the answer is transmitted

    A confirmed detection becomes a 28-byte alert — position, class, confidence — delivered in 14 ms, with the confirming crop 2.5 s behind it. The operator gets a map pin and the evidence to act on it while the aircraft is still flying the pattern.
